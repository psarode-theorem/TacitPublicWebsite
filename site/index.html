The problem
Nobody can say who decided.

Engagements run on meetings, live and recorded, by web or phone, one-to-one or in groups, and on the slides and diagrams that follow. What matters is said in them and never recorded as a decision. Weeks later no one can say which conversation a conclusion came from, who confirmed it, or where the client's material sits. Agents draft in minutes, so the gap grows.

Our hypothesis
A room, not a channel.

One client, one mission, a start and an end. Inside, the Judgment Work pattern runs: agents draft, a named Lead confirms, and the mission commits. The client's context and the provider's method stay on separate shelves. We hypothesise both sides will trust it more than meetings, chat and file shares.

From paper to open source. The Mission Room ideas in the paper are being built as open-source software under Apache-2.0, free to fork for your own kind of work. The paper sets out seven hypotheses we want to test with partners. We are looking for academic feedback, industry collaboration and design partners.

Essay for practitioners · from the working paper

Your judgment is the product. Where does it live?

P. Sarode and R. Nair · Future of Ways of Working, Theoremlabs.io · 5 min read

In short. Expert engagements run on digital meetings, live or recorded, and on the slides, diagrams and chats that follow. None of these record who confirmed what. Agents now draft findings in minutes, so "who decided" gets blurrier. We propose a workspace per engagement that runs the Judgment Work pattern: agents draft and one named person confirms. We want to test whether you and your clients would trust it.

The engagement you already run

You interview a dozen people at a client, on calls and in meetings. You read what documentation exists. You form a view and write it up. Weeks later, someone asks which conversation a conclusion came from, which findings you examined yourself, and where the client's confidential material is now. Most firms can't answer quickly.

Even as offices refill, knowledge work stays digital: web and phone meetings, one-to-one or in groups, live or recorded, with chat and shared files in between. The whiteboard has become Figma or draw.io, and now an AI draws the diagram. The work is richer than ever, and the judgment inside it is no easier to trace.

The most valuable part of the work is the part no tool records.

What agents change

Agents can interview, transcribe and draft. In a field study of 758 consultants, access to a model improved speed and quality on tasks inside its ability, and made people less likely to get the right answer on a task just outside it. The edge of the model's ability isn't visible to the person using it.

That removes much of the junior work, and it raises a harder question than speed: when an agent drafts a finding and a person forwards it, who decided?

Three questions your client will ask

Who owns what? What the client's people said belongs to the client. Your method belongs to you. Where does it live, and who can reach it? Including which model services see it. How can you prove it? In the language their security team already uses.

If a workspace can't answer these in the system itself, not just in a contract, a careful client won't let an agent interview its people.

The “Judgment Work” pattern

Judgment work is work where you are paid to draw out what other people know and then put your name to a verdict: a due-diligence finding, a readiness assessment, a migration plan. It has the same shape every time, and agents make that shape worth writing down. We call it the Judgment Work pattern. It has three moves, which the paper names Draft, Confirm and Commit.

  1. Draft. Agents interview the client's people and draft each finding, with a proposed score and a link to the interview it came from.
  2. Confirm. You, as the named Lead, confirm or change every record. No one else can, and nothing is authoritative until you do.
  3. Commit. The mission closes. The client sees the result read-only, and every finding traces back to its source.

After the pyramid

If agents absorb the junior tasks, the staffing pyramid thins. We describe a smaller team: a Lead who is accountable and the only one who confirms, and an optional Operator who runs the room. The open worry is apprenticeship. Juniors used to learn judgment by doing the work the agents now do.

What we don't know

This is a position paper. We have no field data. We state eight hypotheses, for instance how often leads override an agent's score, and whether interviews change conclusions that documents alone would reach. If the answers are "rarely" and "no", the idea is weaker than we think, and we will say so.

Run one real mission with us

We are looking for five to eight practitioners to run a single engagement in a Mission Room. In return: a co-authored case study and a say in what gets built. Writing a playbook needs your expertise, not code.

Working paper · version 0.8 · 11 October 2026 · draft for comment

Tacit Work Needs a Mission Room

Accountability, Ownership and Data Sovereignty in Agent-Assisted Professional Services
P. Sarode · R. Nair · Adjunct Researchers-in-Residence, Future of Ways of Working, Theoremlabs.io

Expertise engagements between a provider and its client depend on knowledge nobody has written down: expert judgment, and what the client's people know but have never documented. They run on chat, file shares and data rooms, which record neither who knows what, who owns what, where it resides, nor who decided. AI agents can now interview, draft and score at scale, which makes those gaps more costly. We propose the Mission Room: a bounded, staged workspace in which agents draft and a named person confirms, the client's context remains the client's, and the provider's expertise remains the provider's. We argue that such a workspace will not be taken seriously unless ownership is separated and enforced by the system, and unless its answers to data sovereignty, security and privacy can be demonstrated under assurance standards such as SOC 2. We specify the Mission Room through these preconditions, a governance pattern called Draft, Confirm, Commit, a consolidated set of invariants, and an operating model in which a Lead and an optional Operator work with an agent roster in place of the traditional pyramid. We describe an open-source reference implementation, Tacit Chassis, built to hold the invariants, and state eight testable hypotheses. The paper proposes a design and reports no field data.

Investor memo · knowledge management, reborn · October 2026

We have systems of record for customers, money and code. We still have no system for judgment management.

Corporate knowledge management stored what people had written down. The part that matters most, what experts know and decide, was never managed. Agents can now interview, draft and score, and a named person can confirm. That makes a system for judgment management possible, and knowledge management ripe for rebirth.

From paper to open source. The Mission Room ideas in our working paper are being built as open-source software under Apache-2.0, free to fork for any kind of work. We are seeking design partners for the first missions, academic feedback and industry collaboration, and a few investors who see the category early.

01The missing system

Every generation of enterprise software gave one kind of asset a system of record. Judgment, meaning who knows what, what an expert decided on what evidence, and who stood behind it, is still managed in meetings, chat, shared files and slides.

EraWhat it managedThe system of record
1990s, knowledge managementDocuments and written-down know-howIntranets, document and knowledge repositories
2000s to 2010s, enterprise softwareCustomers, money, people, workCRM, ERP, HR systems, issue trackers
2020s, agentsExplicit traces and memory inside one enterpriseKnowledge bases, context graphs, decision logs
NextJudgment across a provider and its client: who knows what, what was decided, on what evidence, confirmed by whomA system for judgment management (the Mission Room, proposed)

02Why knowledge management disappointed, and why it can be reborn

It leaned on capture disciplineKeeping a knowledge base current meant busy experts writing things down. Content went stale, trust fell, contributions dropped.
It managed the easy layerDavenport and Prusak (1998) argued that the human qualities of knowledge, experience and intuition among them, are the most valuable and the hardest to manage.
What changes nowAgents can interview experts and draft the record, so capture no longer depends on experts typing. A named person confirms each record, the Judgment Work pattern, which supplies the trust the old systems lacked.

Lineage: Polanyi (1966) on tacit knowing; Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995) on converting tacit knowledge; Davenport and Prusak (1998) on managing what organizations know. Full references are in the working paper.

03Why now

The theme is hotAccenture Research (March 2026), Gartner (July 2026) and Interloom ($16.5M raised, March 2026) all framed tacit knowledge as the key to agentic AI.
The shape of firms is changingConsulting is moving from the junior-heavy pyramid to small senior teams augmented by agents, and boutiques are winning engagements the large firms can't staff profitably.
Our window is shortMost others capture knowledge inside one enterprise. The provider-and-client version is open for a few quarters, not years.

04The wedge: the boundary, not the memory layer

A Mission Room runs the Judgment Work pattern (Draft, Confirm, Commit) in a governed, time-boxed engagement between two organisations: agents draft, a named Lead confirms, client context stays the client's, provider method stays the provider's. The output is a scored, confirmed decision record with provenance to the source interview. That is a system of record, not a knowledge base. Decision logs and context graphs record what an enterprise does inside its own walls; a Mission Room records expert judgment across the boundary between two organisations.

05Where it bends to financial services

The same base serves any provider that must understand how a client really operates before delivering. Two shapes the paper sketches for financial institutions (illustrative, not built):

Implementation and onboarding are where B2B fintech vendors lose time. A Mission Room is a candidate for the discovery stage of that motion.

06Why open source

Adoption first. Consulting firms and vendors will not put their method into a closed tool owned by a competitor. Apache-2.0, a published playbook format and a rule that no fork can remove the human confirmation gate make the Chassis a standard others can build on. Honest caveat: open-source users will be builders, not consultants, so the hosted route has to serve the rest.

07How it could earn

Business model. Hosting for providers, run by an operating organisation that can meet SOC 2 criteria; premium Mission Room templates; and stewardship of the upstream. Detail is available on request in a conversation, not on the public page.
