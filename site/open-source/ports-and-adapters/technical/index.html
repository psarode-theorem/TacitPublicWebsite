From Mission Room to Fork

Capability Ports and Third-Party Adapters in Tacit Chassis, and How a Fork Is Deployed

Tacit Chassis project

Future of Ways of Working | Theoremlabs.io

15 October 2026 · Draft for comment

tacit.theoremlabs.io/open-source · Comments: research@theoremlabs.io

One chassis holds the rules. Adapters bring the tools. A fork chooses both for one kind of work.

Abstract. The working paper Tacit Work Needs a Mission Room argues for a bounded, staged workspace in which agents draft and a named person confirms. It describes the open-source Tacit Chassis in one section and says that every vendor sits behind a port. This paper makes that sentence concrete for engineers and for the people who would build an industry-specific fork. We distinguish infrastructure ports (database, storage, identity, language model, jobs) from capability ports, which name a job a mission needs done: run a voice interview, draw a diagram, collect a signature, send a risk questionnaire. We set out the contract an adapter for a capability port must keep so that a third-party service can be used without weakening the Chassis invariants, give a catalogue of seven capability ports with the first adapters in our current build scope, and show how a fork composes them. We then describe two deployment models, self-hosted and managed hosting, and what each leaves with the operator. The paper describes a design. It reports no field data, and most of the adapters it names are not yet released.

Keywords: ports and adapters; hexagonal architecture; agentic AI; open source; multi-tenancy; deployment models; professional services

Disclosure. This paper and the open-source reference implementation it describes are supported by Theoremlabs.io, which also develops Tacit Chassis. Third-party products are named to show where an adapter would connect. Naming a product is not a statement that its maker endorses, supports or has agreed to this work. Tacit Chassis is in development and version 0.1 is not yet public.

1. Why this paper, and how to read its status labels

The Mission Room idea is easy to state and easy to doubt: a workspace where agents draft, a named person confirms, and each party keeps what is theirs. Readers who accept it ask a second question: how does this become a product for my kind of work? Our answer is the fork. A fork is a specialization of Tacit Chassis for one industry or one engagement type. It supplies a playbook, stages, roles and record fields, and it supplies the tools the work needs.

That last part decides whether a fork is useful. A third-party risk engagement needs questionnaires and a risk engine. An advisory engagement needs interviews and diagrams. A security-operations review needs a reviewer for scan output. None of these belong in the base. All of them have to enter it without breaking its rules. The mechanism is the port, and this paper is about how far the idea reaches.

The market around this idea is still forming. Several companies and voices are building variants, and no single offering with a settled model has emerged. We read that as a reason to make the base small and the connection points explicit, so that a fork can follow a market without rebuilding the core. This is an evolution of the vision in the working paper, not a change of direction.

Because the paper mixes what exists with what is planned, every statement about a component carries one of three labels.

2. The Chassis in one page

Tacit Chassis is a TypeScript modular monolith: a web application that serves the Studio and the Mission Room, and a set of workspace packages under it. Four rules shape everything else in this paper.

  1. The core is pure. The domain package holds the mission and stage state machine, the Register, scoring and the confirmation rules. It performs no input or output and imports no framework, so the governance invariants can be tested in isolation and forks inherit them unchanged.
  2. Every vendor sits behind a port. Only the adapters package may import a vendor SDK. A build check enforces this. A port describes what the Chassis needs, never a vendor's interface.
  3. Every data access carries a tenant. One tenant is one provider and client pair. A repository or storage call cannot be written without a tenant context, and the database enforces isolation a second time.
  4. Agents draft; the Lead confirms. Nothing an agent or an adapter returns becomes a committed record without an explicit decision by the named Lead.

Today the repository contains the five infrastructure ports and the following adapters: a Postgres adapter with row-level isolation, an adapter for the Anthropic API behind the language-model port, and a fake identity adapter for local runs. The blob port enforces a tenant-prefixed key rule; its storage adapter is still to come. No adapter for a capability port exists in the repository. The current Chassis specification also names storage as the only third-party integration in the base, so everything in Section 4 either lives in forks or enters the base through a specification change (Section 7).

3. Two kinds of port

Infrastructure ports serve the Chassis itself. The database, blob storage, identity, language model and job runner are needed by every mission, whatever the industry. Their adapters are chosen by the host, as in Section 8.

Capability ports serve the work. A capability port names a job a mission needs done and hides who does it. A fork that needs a voice interview calls the Voice Interviewing port; whether a given deployment uses one speech vendor or another is an adapter choice, made per tenant, and does not change the mission, its records or its audit trail.

The distinction matters because the two fail differently and are governed differently. Losing the database stops the product. Losing the diagram service stops a diagram. A capability port also sends mission content to a service the tenant may never have heard of, which is the point at which the ownership and sovereignty invariants of the working paper (O1 to O4, V2) are tested.

4. Capability ports

4.1 Anatomy of a capability port

A capability port is a small typed interface plus a contract. The interface carries a request and returns a result in the port's own vocabulary, for example "interview this subject with this guide" or "render these records as a flow diagram". It never exposes a vendor's parameters. The contract is what lets a service we do not control sit inside a system whose promises we do control.

4.2 The adapter contract

An adapter for a capability port is accepted, in a fork or in the base, only if it keeps the following rules. They restate the invariants of the working paper for third-party code. Rules 2 to 5 are in the code today for the language-model port: a model that declares no identity receives no mission content, a service the tenant's Lead has not approved is refused, each call is logged by size and never by content, and the model's output enters as a draft. The same rules, and the rest of the table, are proposed as the contract for the capability ports.

Table 2: The adapter contract.

#RuleWhat it means in practiceServes
1Tenant firstEvery call takes a tenant context. The adapter builds no request, storage key or vendor account reference without it. Two tenants never share a vendor session.S1, O1
2Declare the processorThe adapter states who it is, where it processes, and how long the vendor retains content. An adapter that declares nothing receives nothing.V2, O4
3Output is a DraftWhatever a vendor returns is marked pending, linked to its source, and carries the vendor's identity. An adapter has no way to confirm a record, set a threshold or commit a stage.G2, G3, G4
4The Lead approves, per tenantA tenant's Lead decides which services may receive its content. Approving a service for one tenant approves it for no other.V2, O6
5Log size, not contentThe event log records the call, the processor and the volume. Mission content never enters the log, an error message or a metric.O4, S3
6Credentials stay with their ownerA credential belongs to the party that holds the vendor contract. How credentials are stored and rotated is a decision still to be recorded.S2, O1
7Fail closedIf the vendor is down or the tenant has not approved it, the mission waits and says so. There is no silent fallback to another vendor.V2, A1
8Ship a fakeEvery port has a deterministic fake adapter, so the whole mission runs locally and in CI without vendor accounts or customer data.Testing
9Pass the port's conformance checksThe conformance package grows port-level checks, run from the adapter's own build: tenant scoping, no confirmation, no content in logs, fail-closed behaviour.All

Two limits deserve plain statement. A rule is only as strong as its check, and rules 5 and 9 can be tested by construction only for what the Chassis observes; what a vendor does with content after it arrives is a matter of the vendor's terms and of the tenant's approval, not of our code. And a port is an abstraction: the narrower the port, the more of a vendor's value it hides, and the wider the port, the harder it is to swap. We expect to revise ports as the first adapters meet real missions.

Three patterns recur, and they are what a fork author should take from the table. Discovery ports bring evidence in; their output is always a draft with a source. Assessment ports propose a judgment, a score or a finding; the working paper's rule that the Lead may override any proposed score applies unchanged. Artifact and commitment ports act on what the Lead has already confirmed, so they can add presentation and signatures but no new claims.

6. From ports to a fork

6.1 The composition root

A fork consumes the Chassis in one of two ways, decided in ADR 0006: it imports the pinned Chassis packages and writes only its extension points, or it hard-forks the reference application and merges upstream at each re-pin. In both cases a fork is assembled in one typed place. The sketch below is illustrative. The shape of createChassis is part of the contract of ADR 0006; the capability-port keys are proposed.

// Illustrative. Capability-port keys are proposed, not released.
const chassis = createChassis({
  playbook: thirdPartyRiskPlaybook,
  stages:   ["Setup", "Discover", "Baseline", "Commit"],
  roles:    { lead: "Engagement Lead", operator: "Analyst" },
  adapters: {
    db: postgres(env), blob: s3(env), identity: sso(env), llm: anthropic(env),
    voice:      elevenlabs(env),   // Voice Interviewing port
    riskEngine: airacloud(env),    // Risk Engine port
    eSignature: docusign(env),     // eSignature port
  },
});
// Startup validation rejects a configuration with no Commit stage,
// or one that lets anyone but the Lead confirm a record.

What a fork may not change is fixed by the extension-point table of the working paper (its Table 9): it may rename stages and roles, define record fields, replace scoring dimensions, add agents and supply playbooks, and it may not remove the Commit stage, let an agent or any role but the Lead confirm, weaken isolation, or disable audit logging. Adapters are one more extension point under the same rule: a fork may choose them freely, and may not choose one that breaks the contract in Table 2.

The business consequence is the reason for the exercise. A vendor with a good product in one of these categories gains a distribution path: every fork that needs the capability can use its adapter, and the tenant's Lead decides whether to approve it. A fork author gains the other side: a capable, governed tool without writing an integration or a compliance story for it. Whether that proves attractive to either is a hypothesis we intend to test with design partners, not a result.

7. How an adapter reaches the base

An adapter written for one fork should not stay trapped there if others can use it. The route follows the rule that anything reusable returns to the Chassis as a specified change and never as a direct merge from a fork.

Two things follow. Until a capability port and its first adapter pass this route, they belong to a fork or to our current build scope and not to the Chassis release. And a vendor, a consultancy or a researcher who wants an adapter included has a defined way to ask: build it against the port, pass the checks, propose the change.

8. Deployment models

The same code runs in two deployment models. They differ in who operates it, where the data lives, and who carries the operating obligations. The Apache License 2.0 intended for the Chassis allows both, and the choice can be made per provider, per client or per mission.

8.1 Self-hosted

The organization runs the Chassis in its own environment, or in its client's data center or cloud account. Theoremlabs.io can help implement or fork it from the open-source code; the organization manages and operates the result. The application is built to run as a container, with Postgres and S3-compatible storage behind the database and blob ports, and the identity port connected to the organization's own sign-in. The adapters are configured by the operator and reach only the services the operator approves.

Self-hosting suits an organization whose clients require data to stay in a named place, one that already runs production software, and one that wants to keep its playbooks and its client records entirely in its own hands. It also lets a provider run missions in the client's own environment. The cost is operational: the operator keeps the system available, patched and recoverable, and gathers its own audit evidence.

8.2 Managed hosting

Theoremlabs.io runs the Chassis for the provider, and also helps implement or fork it. The provider's fork is deployed to a multi-tenant environment, with tenant isolation as the primary control and a United States region planned. The isolation design is that of ADR 0002: one pooled Postgres with a tenant identifier on every content row and row-level security enforced by the database, storage keys prefixed by tenant, and a control plane that can read only metadata such as tenant records and usage counts.

Hosting suits a provider that wants to start without running infrastructure, a provider whose clients accept a vendor-operated service under suitable terms, and the early missions of a fork whose design is still moving. The provider keeps its playbooks and exports, and the same open-source code stays available if it later wants to move to self-hosting. The Chassis is designed so that an organization operating it can meet the SOC 2 Trust Services Criteria and produce the evidence an examination needs. That examination is of the operating organization, not of the software; we have not undergone one and make no SOC 2 claim in this paper.

10. Limitations and open questions

  • No evidence yet. This is a design paper. We have not measured whether capability ports make forks cheaper to build, or whether tenants will approve third-party services.
  • Port shape. Ports designed around a first vendor tend to leak that vendor. The alternates in Table 3 exist to test this, and we expect to change the ports.
  • Vendor terms. The contract governs what the Chassis does. It cannot govern what a vendor does with content after receipt, nor guarantee that vendors will keep the retention and location they declare. The tenant's approval is the control.
  • Specification gap. The current Chassis specification names storage as the only built-in integration. Adding capability ports to the base needs a specification change and a decision record covering credentials, per-tenant approval and the conformance checks.
  • Commercial neutrality. A catalogue of named adapters can look like a marketplace. We have not decided whether, or how, adapters from outside parties are listed, and how we would handle a vendor that competes with an adapter we ship.
  • Agent-to-agent vendors. Some capability vendors are themselves agents. The rule that an adapter cannot confirm assumes the Chassis can see what crosses the port; an agent that calls other services behind the port weakens that assumption.

Comments, critiques and offers to build or review an adapter are welcome at research@theoremlabs.io.
